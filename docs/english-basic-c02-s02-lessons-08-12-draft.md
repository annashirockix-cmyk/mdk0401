# Lesson 8. Passive Voice in Everyday and Scientific Texts

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes
Students will:
1. recognise active and passive voice;
2. form the Present Simple and Past Simple Passive;
3. identify the agent when it is important and omit it when it is unknown or unimportant;
4. understand a short description of how a product is checked;
5. write a short process description.

## 1. Lead-in

Compare:
- Workers check the component.
- The component is checked.

Discuss: Which sentence focuses on the workers? Which focuses on the component? Why might a scientific or technical text use the second sentence?

## 2. Grammar reference

**Passive form:** the correct form of *be* + past participle.

| Tense | Active | Passive |
|---|---|---|
| Present Simple | The team checks the result. | The result is checked. |
| Past Simple | The team checked the result. | The result was checked. |
| Present Perfect | The team has checked the result. | The result has been checked. |
| Future with will | The team will check the result. | The result will be checked. |
| Modal | The team must check the result. | The result must be checked. |

Use *by* when the agent is important: *The report was prepared by the research team.*  
Do not add *by people* when the agent is obvious or irrelevant.

### Practice 1. Change into the passive

1. Scientists measure the temperature.
2. The laboratory recorded the result.
3. The technician has checked the instrument.
4. The team will publish the report.
5. Students must follow the instructions.

### Practice 2. Choose the correct form

1. The samples (is stored / are stored) in labelled containers.
2. The first test (was completed / were completed) yesterday.
3. The results (has been checked / have been checked).
4. The equipment (must inspect / must be inspected) before use.

## 3. Reading: how a sample is checked

Read the process description.

**A simple quality check**

First, a sample is given an identification number. Its condition is recorded, and the relevant measurement is taken with an appropriate instrument. The result is compared with the stated requirement. If the value is unclear or outside the expected range, the result is checked again and the responsible instructor is informed. Finally, the record is completed and stored according to the classroom procedure.

### Reading tasks
1. What happens first?
2. Why is an identification number used?
3. What is compared with the requirement?
4. When is the instructor informed?
5. Find four passive forms.
6. Why is it useful to record the process?

## 4. Listening: describing a checking procedure

**Before you listen:** *sample, label, measure, compare, record, repeat*. Predict the order of the steps.

**First listening — gist:** What is being explained?
A) A simple checking procedure. B) A college trip. C) A sports event.

**Second listening — details**
1. What is added first? A) an identification label B) a price tag C) a timetable
2. What is recorded? A) the measurement B) the weather forecast C) the student's address
3. What happens if the result is unclear? A) it is checked again B) it is ignored C) the sample is taken home
4. What happens at the end? A) the record is completed B) the label is removed without a note C) the result is guessed

**Recording script — reveal after listening**

**Student:** Could you explain the order of the checking procedure?

**Instructor:** First, the sample is labelled with an identification number. Then its condition is recorded.

**Student:** Is the measurement taken after that?

**Instructor:** Yes. The result is compared with the stated requirement. If it is unclear, the measurement is checked again.

**Student:** And who receives the record?

**Instructor:** The completed record is stored according to the classroom procedure, and any problem is reported to the responsible instructor.

**Student:** So the sample is labelled, checked, measured and recorded.

**Instructor:** Correct. Do not guess a result or change the procedure.

**Language focus:** *is labelled, is recorded, is compared, is checked* — Present Simple Passive.

**After listening:** In pairs, describe a familiar classroom process in five steps. Use at least four passive forms.

## 5. Speaking and writing

Describe how a library book is borrowed, how a classroom presentation is prepared or how a sample is checked. Write 80–100 words and use:
- three Present Simple Passive forms;
- one Past Simple Passive form;
- sequence markers *first, next, then, finally*.

## 6. Self-check and key

1. Complete: “The report ______ (prepare) every Friday.”
2. Change: “The team recorded the data.”
3. Why might the agent be omitted?
4. Listening answers: gist A; details 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 4 A.

**Teacher key:** 1 is prepared; 2 The data was recorded by the team; 3 because the action/result is more important or the agent is unknown/unimportant. Practice 1: 1 The temperature is measured by scientists. 2 The result was recorded by the laboratory. 3 The instrument has been checked by the technician. 4 The report will be published by the team. 5 The instructions must be followed by students. Practice 2: 1 are stored; 2 was completed; 3 have been checked; 4 must be inspected. Reading answers follow the passage. Writing rubric (8): process accuracy 2, passive forms 3, sequence 1, vocabulary 1, clarity 1.

**Independent work:** find five passive sentences in a teacher-provided safe, non-confidential information text. Rewrite two in the active voice if the agent is known.

# Lesson 9. Linking Words and Building a Coherent Paragraph

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes
Students will:
1. identify the function of common linking words;
2. organise sentences into a logical paragraph;
3. use reference words and repeated key terms to maintain cohesion;
4. listen for a speaker's argument and supporting reasons;
5. write a coherent paragraph of 100–120 words.

## 1. Lead-in

Which is easier to understand?

A) “The system saves time. It needs maintenance. It can produce errors. The operator checks it.”

B) “The system can save time; however, it still needs maintenance. It may produce errors, so the operator must check its status.”

Explain how the linking words help the reader.

## 2. Linking words

| Function | Linking words | Example |
|---|---|---|
| addition | and, also, furthermore | The process is faster. It is also easier to repeat. |
| contrast | but, however, although | The tool is useful; however, it has limits. |
| reason | because, since | The test was repeated because the result was unclear. |
| result | so, therefore, as a result | The data was incomplete, so the team checked it again. |
| example | for example, such as | Some sensors measure temperature, for example. |
| sequence | first, next, finally | First, the sample is labelled. |
| conclusion | in conclusion, overall | Overall, the method is effective when used correctly. |

Punctuation:
- *However,* often begins a new sentence or follows a semicolon.
- *Although* introduces a dependent clause: *Although the method is simple, it requires care.*
- *Because* introduces a reason clause.
- *Therefore* introduces a result or conclusion.

### Practice 1. Choose the best linker

1. The system is useful; ______, it needs regular checks. (however / because)
2. The result was unclear, ______ the team repeated the measurement. (so / although)
3. ______ the equipment was not available, the group used the approved alternative. (Because / However)
4. The report includes diagrams. It ______ contains a glossary. (also / although)
5. ______, the proposal should be tested before it is adopted. (In conclusion / For example)

## 3. Reading: a coherent paragraph

Read:

“Digital records can make information easier to find. For example, a student can search for a document by its title or date. However, digital storage does not remove the need for accurate file names and regular backups. If documents are saved in unclear folders, users may waste time looking for them. Therefore, a simple naming system should be agreed by the group. In conclusion, digital tools are most useful when people follow a clear procedure.”

### Reading tasks
1. Underline the topic sentence.
2. Find the example.
3. Which sentence introduces a limitation?
4. Which sentence gives a recommendation?
5. What does *therefore* signal?
6. Suggest a suitable title.

## 4. Paragraph structure

A clear paragraph usually has:
1. **Topic sentence** — introduces the main idea.
2. **Supporting detail** — explains or gives evidence.
3. **Example** — makes the point concrete.
4. **Concluding sentence** — summarises or gives an implication.

### Practice 2. Put the sentences in order

a) Therefore, students should check the source and date before using the information.  
b) Online information is easy to access.  
c) However, not every page is accurate or up to date.  
d) For example, an old technical guide may describe a procedure that has changed.

## 5. Listening: improving a short report

**Before you listen:** *topic sentence, reason, example, contrast, conclusion*. Predict what feedback a teacher might give about an unclear paragraph.

**First listening — gist:** What is the teacher helping a student do?
A) Organise a paragraph. B) Repair a device. C) Buy a ticket.

**Second listening**
1. What is missing from the student's paragraph? A) a clear main idea B) a photograph C) a signature
2. What should come after the main idea? A) a reason or supporting detail B) an unrelated story C) the bibliography only
3. Which linker introduces contrast? A) however B) because C) for example
4. What should the final sentence do? A) return to the main point B) introduce a new unrelated topic C) repeat the title only

**Recording script**

**Teacher:** Your paragraph contains useful ideas, but the order is not clear.

**Student:** Should I start with the example?

**Teacher:** Start with the main idea. Then explain why it matters. After that, give an example.

**Student:** I also want to mention a limitation.

**Teacher:** Good. Use *however* to introduce the contrast. Finally, write a sentence that returns to your main point.

**Student:** So the paragraph will move from the main idea to a reason, then an example, a limitation and a conclusion.

**Teacher:** Exactly. Each sentence should help develop the same central idea.

**Language focus:** *after that* signals sequence; *however* signals contrast; *finally* signals the last step.

**After listening:** Take a disorganised paragraph supplied by the teacher and reorder it. Explain your choices.

## 6. Writing task

Write 100–120 words on one topic:
- Why students should check online sources;
- Benefits and limitations of digital learning;
- Why regular maintenance matters.

Include a topic sentence, two supporting details, an example, a contrast linker and a concluding sentence.

## 7. Self-check and key

1. Which linker introduces contrast?
2. Which linker introduces a reason?
3. What is the function of a topic sentence?
4. Practice 1: 1 however; 2 so; 3 Because; 4 also; 5 In conclusion.
5. Practice 2: b → c → d → a.
6. Listening: gist A; details 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 4 A.

**Writing rubric (8):** topic sentence 1; supporting ideas 2; example 1; contrast 1; conclusion 1; cohesion and punctuation 1; grammar/vocabulary 1.

**Independent work:** revise the paragraph and highlight each linker by function: addition, contrast, reason, result, example or conclusion.

# Lesson 10. Climate, Resources and Sustainable Development

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes
Students will:
1. understand vocabulary for climate, energy, resources and sustainability;
2. distinguish weather from climate;
3. read a short text for main ideas and supporting details;
4. use *too, enough, fewer, less, more* in context;
5. discuss practical resource-saving measures and their limitations.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
- What is the difference between weather and climate?
- Which resources are used in a college building?
- What can students do to avoid wasting electricity, water and materials?
- Why should environmental claims be supported by reliable data?

## 2. Vocabulary

| English | Russian | Example |
|---|---|---|
| climate | климат | Climate describes long-term patterns. |
| weather | погода | Weather can change from day to day. |
| resource | ресурс | Water is an important resource. |
| renewable | возобновляемый | Wind is a renewable energy source. |
| non-renewable | невозобновляемый | Coal is a non-renewable resource. |
| consumption | потребление | Energy consumption varies by season. |
| efficiency | эффективность | Efficiency can reduce unnecessary use. |
| sustainable development | устойчивое развитие | Sustainable development considers long-term needs. |
| emissions | выбросы | Some energy sources produce emissions. |
| insulation | теплоизоляция | Insulation can reduce heat loss. |
| to conserve | беречь, сохранять | We should conserve water. |
| to waste | тратить впустую | Do not waste materials. |
| to reuse | использовать повторно | Reuse materials when it is safe and appropriate. |
| to compare | сравнивать | Compare readings over the same period. |

## 3. Reading: making resource use more efficient

Read:

“Many organisations review how they use energy, water and materials. A useful review begins with reliable information: bills, meter readings, maintenance records or a simple observation log. The next step is to identify where resources may be wasted. For example, a building may lose heat through poorly maintained doors or windows. A workshop may use more compressed air than necessary because of a leak. However, a proposed improvement must be checked by the responsible staff. A student should never change equipment settings or repair a system without permission. Sustainable improvement combines evidence, appropriate technology and responsible behaviour.”

### Reading tasks
1. What should a review begin with?
2. Name two sources of information.
3. Give two examples of possible waste.
4. Why must a proposed improvement be checked?
5. Which sentence sets a safety boundary?
6. What three elements does the final sentence combine?

## 4. Grammar and comparison

- **fewer** + countable plural nouns: fewer paper sheets;
- **less** + uncountable nouns: less water;
- **more** + countable or uncountable nouns: more efficient equipment / more energy;
- **too** + adjective/adverb: too expensive;
- **adjective + enough**: efficient enough;
- **enough + noun**: enough information.

### Practice 1. Complete

1. We should use ______ paper (less / fewer).
2. The building needs ______ energy in winter (less / fewer).
3. There are ______ unnecessary printouts this term (less / fewer).
4. The report does not contain ______ information (enough / too).
5. This proposal is ______ expensive for our budget (too / enough).

## 5. Listening: reviewing energy use

**Before you listen:** *meter reading, consumption, insulation, compare, approval*. Predict what information is needed before proposing an energy-saving measure.

**First listening — gist:** What are the speakers doing?
A) Reviewing energy use and suggesting a measured improvement. B) Planning a concert. C) Describing a holiday.

**Second listening**
1. What information do they compare? A) readings from two periods B) two student names C) two photographs
2. What possible issue do they mention? A) heat loss near a door B) a broken classroom clock C) a missing textbook
3. Who must approve building changes? A) responsible staff B) any student C) a visitor
4. What should happen after an approved change? A) the results should be measured again B) all records should be deleted C) no one should check it

**Recording script**

**Student:** We have compared the energy readings from two months.

**Instructor:** What did you notice?

**Student:** The use was higher during the colder period. We also noticed that the entrance door does not close properly.

**Instructor:** That may contribute to heat loss, but we need more information before drawing a conclusion.

**Student:** Could we suggest that the maintenance team check the door?

**Instructor:** Yes. Do not try to repair it yourselves. The responsible staff must inspect it and approve any work.

**Student:** After the repair, should we compare the readings again?

**Instructor:** Yes, but compare similar periods and consider the weather. That will make the review more useful.

**Language focus:** *may contribute to* expresses a possibility, not certainty; *must inspect* expresses a requirement; *compare similar periods* describes a fair comparison.

**After listening:** Make a three-step plan: information needed, person responsible for approval, method of evaluating the result.

## 6. Speaking and writing

In groups, propose one resource-saving measure for a college room. Explain:
- the problem;
- what evidence is needed;
- who must approve the action;
- how the result can be evaluated;
- one possible limitation.

Write 100–120 words using *less, fewer, enough* and *however*.

## 7. Self-check and key

1. What is the difference between weather and climate?
2. Complete: “We need ______ paper and ______ energy.”
3. Why should similar periods be compared?
4. Practice 1: 1 less; 2 less; 3 fewer; 4 enough; 5 too.
5. Reading: reliable information; bills/meter readings/records/logs; examples include heat loss and compressed-air leaks; responsible staff must check safety and technical suitability; students must not change settings or repair systems; evidence, appropriate technology and responsible behaviour.
6. Listening: gist A; details 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 4 A.

**Writing rubric (8):** problem and evidence 2; action and approval 2; evaluation 1; target vocabulary 1; coherence 1; accuracy 1.

**Independent work:** prepare a short comparison of two resource-use options using only figures supplied by the teacher. Do not invent consumption data.

# Lesson 11. Science and Technology in Everyday Life

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes
Students will:
1. understand vocabulary for devices, data, applications and innovation;
2. identify a technology's purpose, benefits and limitations;
3. use defining relative clauses with *which, that, who*;
4. listen for a product explanation and a qualification;
5. give a short balanced presentation about a technology.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
- Which technologies help you study?
- What is one benefit and one limitation of a smartphone?
- Why should users protect personal information?
- Does a new technology always solve the problem it was designed for?

## 2. Vocabulary

| English | Russian | Example |
|---|---|---|
| device | устройство | A sensor is a measuring device. |
| application | приложение; применение | The application stores information. |
| data | данные | The device collects data. |
| digital | цифровой | Digital records are easy to search. |
| network | сеть | Devices may communicate through a network. |
| user | пользователь | The user controls access. |
| interface | интерфейс | The interface displays information. |
| innovation | инновация | Innovation can improve a process. |
| reliable | надёжный | A reliable device gives consistent results. |
| limitation | ограничение | Every system has limitations. |
| privacy | конфиденциальность | Privacy is important when data is collected. |
| security | безопасность, защищённость | Security measures protect information. |
| to collect | собирать | Sensors collect measurements. |
| to process | обрабатывать | Software processes data. |
| to update | обновлять | Users should update software responsibly. |

## 3. Grammar: defining relative clauses

A relative clause gives essential information about a person or thing.

- A sensor is a device **that measures** a physical quantity.
- A programmer is a person **who writes** software.
- A database is a system **which stores** organised information.

Use:
- **who** for people;
- **which** for things;
- **that** for people or things in defining clauses.

### Practice 1. Join the sentences

1. A sensor is a device. It measures a condition.
2. A programmer is a specialist. The specialist writes code.
3. A database is a collection. The collection stores information.
4. A warning is a message. The message tells the user about a possible problem.

## 4. Reading: a connected device

Read:

“A connected device can collect information and send it to another system. For example, a smart meter records resource use and displays the readings to a user. The information may help people understand patterns and identify unusual changes. However, the value of the data depends on the quality of the measurement and the way it is interpreted. Connected devices can also create privacy and security questions. Users should understand what information is collected, who can access it and how it is protected.”

### Reading tasks
1. What can a connected device do?
2. What does the example smart meter record?
3. How can information help users?
4. What does the value of data depend on?
5. Name two questions related to connected devices.
6. Find one sentence that introduces a limitation.

## 5. Listening: explaining a connected sensor

**Before you listen:** *collect, transmit, display, reliable, access, privacy*. Predict what a user should know before using a connected sensor.

**First listening — gist:** What is being explained?
A) The purpose and limitations of a connected sensor. B) A cooking recipe. C) A college timetable.

**Second listening**
1. What does the sensor collect? A) measurements B) passwords for unrelated accounts C) music
2. Where can the user see the readings? A) in an application B) on a paper ticket only C) in a library book
3. What should the user check? A) measurement quality and data access B) the colour of the packaging only C) the device's weight only
4. What is one limitation? A) readings may be inaccurate or incomplete B) every reading is always correct C) data never needs protection

**Recording script**

**Student:** What does this connected sensor do?

**Technician:** It collects measurements and sends them to an application, where the readings are displayed.

**Student:** Can we use the readings to make decisions?

**Technician:** They can be useful, but first you should understand how the sensor works and whether the measurements are reliable.

**Student:** What about the information in the application?

**Technician:** Check who can access it and how it is protected. Do not share account details or personal information unnecessarily.

**Student:** So the device is useful, but the data must be interpreted carefully.

**Technician:** Exactly. Technology provides information; people still need to evaluate it.

**Language focus:** *where the readings are displayed* — relative clause; *can be useful* — possibility; *must be interpreted* — passive with modal.

**After listening:** Present a device in four parts: purpose, information collected, benefit and limitation.

## 6. Speaking and writing

Prepare a 90-second presentation about a technology used in education, transport, communication or manufacturing. Include:
- what it is;
- what it does;
- who uses it;
- one benefit;
- one limitation;
- one privacy or safety consideration if relevant.

Write a 100-word profile using two relative clauses.

## 7. Self-check and key

1. Which relative pronoun is normally used for people?
2. Join: “A database is a system. It stores information.”
3. Why should data quality be checked?
4. Practice 1: 1 A sensor is a device that/which measures a condition. 2 A programmer is a specialist who writes code. 3 A database is a collection that/which stores information. 4 A warning is a message that/which tells the user about a possible problem.
5. Reading: collects and sends information; resource use; identify patterns/unusual changes; measurement quality and interpretation; privacy and security; “However…” sentence.
6. Listening: gist A; details 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 4 A.

**Presentation rubric (8):** purpose 1; operation 2; benefit 1; limitation 1; relevant consideration 1; relative clause 1; clarity 1.

**Independent work:** write a short comparison of two technologies using *where, which* or *who* correctly.

# Lesson 12. Expressing an Opinion: Short Essay and Presentation

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes
Students will:
1. distinguish an opinion from a supporting reason and an example;
2. use cautious language to avoid unsupported generalisations;
3. organise a short essay with an introduction, body and conclusion;
4. listen to a short opinion and identify its claim and support;
5. deliver a structured two-minute presentation.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
- Should every process be automated?
- Is digital information always more reliable than printed information?
- Should colleges introduce more project-based learning?
- What makes an argument convincing?

A strong opinion is not simply a strong statement. It needs reasons, examples and acknowledgement of limitations.

## 2. Useful language

| Purpose | Useful expressions |
|---|---|
| state an opinion | I believe that… / In my view… |
| give a reason | This is because… / One reason is… |
| give an example | For example… / For instance… |
| add a point | Furthermore… / In addition… |
| show contrast | However… / On the other hand… |
| qualify a claim | In some cases… / It may… / This depends on… |
| conclude | To sum up… / In conclusion… |

Avoid unsupported absolutes such as *everyone, always, never, completely solves*. Prefer precise and qualified language.

## 3. Reading: a balanced opinion

Read:

“Automation can improve some production processes because machines can repeat programmed actions consistently. It may also reduce the need for people to perform certain repetitive or physically demanding tasks. However, automation does not remove every problem. Equipment must be designed, checked and maintained, and people need training to respond to faults. In my view, automation is most useful when it supports skilled workers and is introduced with clear procedures. For example, a monitoring system can help an operator notice an unusual reading, but the operator must still interpret the information and follow the approved response.”

### Reading tasks
1. What is the writer's opinion?
2. Give two benefits mentioned.
3. What limitation is introduced by *however*?
4. What example supports the writer's opinion?
5. Which sentence is the conclusion?
6. Does the writer claim that automation is always beneficial? Explain.

## 4. Listening: building an argument

**Before you listen:** *claim, reason, example, limitation, conclusion*. Predict the structure of a short classroom opinion.

**First listening — gist:** What is the speaker arguing?
A) Technology should support people, but it needs responsible use. B) All technology should be banned. C) Students should not study science.

**Second listening**
1. What is the speaker's main claim? A) technology can be useful when used responsibly B) technology always works perfectly C) technology has no limitations
2. What reason is given? A) it can help people find and process information B) it removes the need to learn C) it makes every decision automatically
3. What limitation is mentioned? A) information can be inaccurate B) devices never fail C) all data is private by default
4. What does the speaker recommend? A) check information and use judgement B) accept every result C) ignore instructions

**Recording script**

**Student:** In my view, digital tools can make learning more flexible. Students can access materials and review information outside the classroom.

**Teacher:** That is your main claim. Can you support it with a reason?

**Student:** Yes. For example, a recorded explanation can be reviewed more than once, which may help students understand a difficult topic.

**Teacher:** Good. What is one limitation?

**Student:** Online information is not always accurate, and students may become distracted. Therefore, they should check sources and use digital tools for a clear purpose.

**Teacher:** Finish with your conclusion.

**Student:** To sum up, digital tools are useful when they support learning, but they do not replace careful thinking or communication with a teacher.

**Language focus:** *in my view* states an opinion; *for example* introduces support; *however* or *but* introduces a limitation; *to sum up* signals the conclusion.

**After listening:** Students identify the claim, reason, example, limitation and conclusion. Then they give a 60-second opinion on a related topic.

## 5. Writing: a short opinion essay

Write 120–150 words on one topic:
- “Automation should support skilled workers.”
- “Digital learning is useful when it is carefully organised.”
- “Environmental improvements should be based on evidence.”

Structure:
1. Introduction: introduce the issue and state your opinion.
2. Body paragraph: give a reason and an example.
3. Limitation: acknowledge another side or a condition.
4. Conclusion: restate your position in different words.

### Planning grid

| Question | Notes |
|---|---|
| What is the issue? | |
| What is my opinion? | |
| What is my strongest reason? | |
| What example supports it? | |
| What limitation should I acknowledge? | |
| What is my conclusion? | |

## 6. Speaking: two-minute presentation

Use six cue cards or notes:
1. topic;
2. opinion;
3. reason;
4. example;
5. limitation;
6. conclusion.

Do not read a full essay aloud. Speak from notes and use signposting phrases.

## 7. Self-check and key

1. What is the difference between a claim and an example?
2. Which expression introduces a limitation?
3. Why should an opinion essay include reasons?
4. Reading: opinion — automation is most useful when it supports skilled workers and is introduced with clear procedures; benefits — consistency and reduction of some repetitive/demanding tasks; limitation — design, checking, maintenance and training remain necessary; example — monitoring system; conclusion — final sentence; the writer does not claim automation is always beneficial.
5. Listening: gist A; details 1 A, 2 A, 3 A, 4 A.

**Essay rubric (10 points):** clear position 2; relevant reasons 2; example 1; limitation/counterpoint 1; logical structure 1; linking words 1; vocabulary 1; grammar and accuracy 1.

**Presentation rubric (8 points):** clear opinion 1; reason 1; example 1; limitation 1; conclusion 1; organisation 1; intelligibility 1; appropriate pace 1.

**Independent work:** revise the essay using teacher feedback. Underline the claim, circle the example and put a box around the conclusion.
