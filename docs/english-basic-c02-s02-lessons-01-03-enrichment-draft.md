# Lesson 1. Conditionals and Reported Speech: Explaining Causes, Results and Instructions

**Source link:** Basic 4.1 — Grammar: Conditionals & Reported Speech  
**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students will be able to:
1. choose the First, Second or Third Conditional for a real, hypothetical or past-unreal situation;
2. explain cause and result using if-clauses;
3. report statements, requests and questions using an appropriate reporting verb;
4. preserve the meaning of a technical or classroom instruction when reporting it;
5. listen for a problem, its cause and the action requested.

## 1. Lead-in: cause and consequence

Discuss in pairs:
- What happens if a student forgets to check a drawing revision?
- What would you do if a measurement seemed incorrect?
- What could have happened if a warning had been ignored?
- Why is it important to report an instruction accurately?

Complete the chain orally:

**If a document is out of date, …**  
**If I were responsible for the inspection, …**  
**If the team had checked the units, …**

## 2. Grammar focus A: conditionals

Conditionals connect a condition with a possible or imagined result.

| Type | Form | Use | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zero | If + Present Simple, Present Simple | general facts and regular results | If ice gets warm, it melts. |
| First | If + Present Simple, will + verb | real future possibility | If the team checks the file, it will find the latest revision. |
| Second | If + Past Simple, would + verb | imaginary or unlikely present/future | If I had more time, I would review the whole report. |
| Third | If + Past Perfect, would have + V3 | unreal past and imagined past result | If they had checked the units, they would have avoided the error. |

### Important points

- Do not normally put **will** directly after *if* in a standard First Conditional:  
  Correct: *If the bus is late, I will call the instructor.*  
  Incorrect: *If the bus will be late…*
- In the Second Conditional, **were** is often used for all persons in formal or careful English: *If I were the team leader…*
- The Third Conditional describes an alternative past that did not happen: *If we had saved the file, we would not have lost the changes.*
- The if-clause may come first or second. Use a comma when it comes first.

### Practice 1. Choose the correct form

1. If the temperature (rises / will rise), the material expands.
2. If the team finishes early, it (will review / would review) the report.
3. If I (am / were) the supervisor, I would ask for another measurement.
4. If they had followed the checklist, they (would avoid / would have avoided) the mistake.
5. If the file is not available, we (will use / would have used) the printed copy.

### Practice 2. Complete the sentences

1. If a student does not understand an instruction, …
2. If I had a free week, …
3. If the operator had checked the setting, …
4. If people use less energy, …
5. If the weather is unsuitable for travel, …

Students must use a different conditional type in at least four answers.

## 3. Grammar focus B: reported speech

Reported speech tells another person what someone said. The reporting verb is often in the past.

| Direct speech | Reported speech |
|---|---|
| “The file is ready,” she said. | She said (that) the file was ready. |
| “We checked the measurements,” they said. | They said (that) they had checked the measurements. |
| “I will call you,” he said. | He said (that) he would call me. |
| “I can open the document,” she said. | She said (that) she could open the document. |

### Common backshift

| Direct form | Common reported form |
|---|---|
| Present Simple | Past Simple |
| Present Continuous | Past Continuous |
| Present Perfect | Past Perfect |
| Past Simple | Past Perfect |
| will | would |
| can | could |
| must | had to (for obligation in many contexts) |

Backshift is common when the reporting verb is past and the speaker reports from a later point. It is not automatic in every context: a fact that is still true may remain in the present.

### Reported questions

- Direct: “Where is the updated drawing?”  
  Reported: She asked **where the updated drawing was**.
- Direct: “Has the report been sent?”  
  Reported: He asked **if the report had been sent**.

Use statement word order after the question word or *if/whether*. Do not use *do/does/did* in the reported question.

### Reported requests and instructions

- “Please check the date.” → The instructor asked us **to check the date**.
- “Do not change the settings.” → The supervisor told the team **not to change the settings**.

### Practice 3. Report the statements

1. “The results are ready,” Anna said.
2. “We have checked the latest revision,” the students said.
3. “I will send the table tomorrow,” Pavel said.
4. “The device can store the data,” the technician said.

### Practice 4. Report the questions and instructions

## 4. Reading: a near miss caused by an unclear instruction

Read the short case.

**A missing unit**

During a classroom measurement exercise, a student copied a value from a worksheet but did not write the unit. Another student noticed that the drawing used millimetres, while an older example in the folder used inches. The group stopped and asked the instructor to clarify the document.

The instructor explained that the current worksheet used millimetres throughout. The older example had been kept in the folder for comparison and was not part of the current task. The group corrected the record and added the document revision to its notes.

The instructor later said that the students had acted correctly because they had stopped instead of guessing. She reminded the group that a number without a unit can be misleading. She also asked students to report unclear or conflicting documents before continuing.

### Reading tasks

1. What information was missing from the student's record?
2. Why were the students unsure?
3. What did the group do before continuing?
4. What did the instructor say the group had done correctly?
5. Find one sentence in reported speech.
6. What general lesson can be drawn from the case?
7. Is the case an instruction for inspecting real aircraft parts? Explain.

## 5. Listening: reporting a problem to an instructor

**Before you listen**

Key words:
- mismatch — несоответствие;
- previous revision — предыдущая редакция;
- clarify — уточнить;
- record — запись / фиксировать;
- proceed — продолжать.

Predict: What details should a student include when reporting a mismatch between two documents?

**First listening — gist**

Why does the student contact the instructor?

A) Two versions of a worksheet contain different information.  
B) The student wants to change the lesson time.  
C) The student has forgotten the classroom number.

**Second listening — details**

1. What is different in the two files?
   A) the revision date  B) the student's name  C) the room colour
2. What does the student ask the instructor to confirm?
   A) which version is current  B) who bought the folder  C) where to have lunch
3. What does the instructor tell the student to do?
   A) wait for clarification and not guess  
   B) choose any version  
   C) delete both files
4. What will the student do after the call?
   A) update the group  B) leave the college  C) change the measurement units independently

**Recording script — reveal after the tasks**

**Student:** Excuse me. I am checking the worksheet for tomorrow, and I have found two versions in the folder.  
**Instructor:** What is different?  
**Student:** The revision dates are different. The newer file also has one extra instruction. Could you confirm which version we should use?  
**Instructor:** Use the file marked Revision C. I uploaded it yesterday.  
**Student:** Let me check that I understood. You said that Revision C is the current file and that we should not use Revision B.  
**Instructor:** That's right. Please tell the group and ask everyone to replace the older copy. If anything is still unclear, come and see me before the exercise.  
**Student:** Thank you. I will update the group now.

**Language focus**
- *I have found two versions* — present perfect for a relevant discovery.
- *Could you confirm…?* — polite request.
- *You said that Revision C was the current file* — reported statement.
- *The instructor told us not to use Revision B* — reported instruction.

**After listening**

In pairs, Student A reports a document problem. Student B asks two questions and gives a clear instruction. Student A then reports the instruction to a third student using reported speech.

## 4. Grammar and language: cause, result and recommendation

### Cause and result

| Function | Linker | Example |
|---|---|---|
| cause | because / because of | The river was polluted because of a chemical leak. |
| result | so / therefore / as a result | The process used less energy, so emissions were lower. |
| contrast | however / although | Recycling is useful; however, it does not solve every problem. |
| purpose | to / in order to | We switched off unused lights to save energy. |

Remember:
- **because + clause:** because the machine was inefficient;
- **because of + noun phrase:** because of inefficient equipment.

### Recommendations

- **should** — advice: We should avoid unnecessary printing.
- **must** — strong requirement: Students must follow the workshop's waste rules.
- **can** — possibility/ability: We can reuse suitable packaging.
- **mustn't** — prohibition: Students mustn't mix unknown substances.

### Practice 2. Choose the correct word

1. The team reduced paper use (because / because of) it introduced digital worksheets.
2. The equipment was old; (therefore / although), it used more energy.
3. Students (should / mustn't) follow the approved waste procedure.
4. We turned off the lights (to / because) save electricity.
5. (Although / Because of) recycling is useful, it cannot prevent all pollution.

# Lesson 3. The Speed Limit: Reading a Scientific Explanation

**Source link:** Basic 4.3 — Reading Practice: The Speed Limit  
**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students will be able to:
1. identify the main claim and supporting explanation in a popular-science text;
2. understand key vocabulary about speed, motion, signals and experiments;
3. scan a text for comparisons, examples and conclusions;
4. distinguish a scientific explanation from an unsupported assumption;
5. summarise a short scientific text in their own words.

## 1. Lead-in: speed and communication

Discuss:
- What is the difference between speed and distance?
- What travels faster: a car, an aircraft or a signal carried by light?
- Why do scientists use experiments?
- Why should a popular-science text explain unfamiliar ideas with examples?

Do not try to calculate physical values in this language lesson. The aim is reading comprehension and accurate explanation.

### Scientific reading note

The textbook passage is a historical/popular-science reading. In contemporary physics, the statement about the speed of light is usually expressed more precisely: in vacuum, the speed of light is the invariant speed that limits the propagation of causal influences and information. This lesson does not ask students to prove relativity; it asks them to identify the author's argument and read scientific explanations carefully.

## 4. Reading skill: fact, example, conclusion

- **Fact or established principle:** a statement supported by scientific evidence.
- **Example:** a comparison used to make an idea easier to understand.
- **Conclusion:** a statement reached from evidence or reasoning.
- **Assumption:** an idea accepted without sufficient support in the text.
