# Technical English — Course 3, Semester 2 — Lessons 9–12
## Cutting tools, tolerances, inspection and safe workshop communication

**Status:** original draft lessons for the provisional five-year course map; not imported into the portal and not approved as official programme content.  
**Level:** B1–B1+ ESP.  
**Professional focus:** machine-tool operators and technologists, with shared tasks for programmers and electricians.  
**Audio:** original Spotlight-style listening scripts for future recording; no MP3 files or player are included.  
**Safety boundary:** examples teach English and document reading only. They are not machining parameters, acceptance criteria, maintenance instructions or permission to operate equipment.

## Block outcomes

Students will be able to:
- name common cutting-tool types and describe tool wear in general terms;
- understand basic language for tool data without inventing cutting parameters;
- distinguish nominal size, tolerance, fit and surface-finish terminology;
- read a simplified measurement record and describe why instrument status matters;
- understand the purpose of a first-piece inspection record;
- give a clear workshop handover and use stop-work language when information is unclear or a hazard is noticed.

# Lesson 9. Cutting Tools, Tool Wear and Tool Data

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students will:
1. identify common cutting-tool terms in introductory technical texts;
2. distinguish a cutting tool, tool holder, insert and workpiece;
3. understand general vocabulary for wear, damage and tool life;
4. read a fictional tool-data record and identify its fields;
5. listen to a tool-condition handover and identify reported facts;
6. explain why tool selection and replacement must follow approved documentation and supervision.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
- What is the difference between a cutting tool and a workpiece?
- Why can a tool condition affect the quality of a machined surface?
- What information might be recorded about a tool?
- Why should a student not choose cutting parameters by guessing?

## 3. Reading: tool condition and documentation

Read:

“A cutting tool is selected for a particular operation and material according to approved process information. A tool assembly may include a holder and a replaceable insert. During use, the cutting edge can experience wear. The type and rate of wear depend on the tool material, workpiece material, machine, process and operating conditions.

A tool-condition record may identify the tool, holder, insert type, inspection date and observed condition. It may also refer to a tool-life policy or approved process document. A visual description such as ‘edge damage observed’ reports an observation; it does not by itself authorise a replacement method or establish a machining parameter. If a tool appears damaged or its identity is uncertain, the condition must be reported and the approved workshop procedure followed.”

### First reading — gist

A) The text explains tool vocabulary and the purpose of condition records.  
B) The text provides cutting speeds for every material.  
C) The text gives permission to change a tool without supervision.

### Second reading — details

2. Name three factors that can affect tool wear.
3. What information may a condition record contain?
4. Does the phrase “edge damage observed” give a replacement procedure?
5. What should happen if tool identity is uncertain?

## 4. Language focus: describing condition objectively

Useful phrases:
- **The tool is identified as…**
- **Wear is visible on…**
- **The edge appears damaged.**
- **The condition was recorded on…**
- **The observation has been reported to…**
- **The record does not specify…**

Avoid unsupported conclusions:
- Instead of *“The tool is safe”*, say *“No visible damage was noted in this classroom example.”*
- Instead of *“The tool must be replaced now”*, say *“The reported condition should be reviewed under the approved procedure.”*

# Lesson 10. Tolerances, Fits and Surface Finish

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students will:
1. understand the terms nominal dimension, actual size, tolerance and limit;
2. recognise introductory language for clearance, interference and transition fits;
3. understand that surface finish describes surface texture and is specified by a controlled document;
4. read a simplified classroom tolerance table;
5. listen to a discussion about interpreting a dimension and its tolerance;
6. explain why an isolated measurement cannot establish acceptance without the applicable specification and method.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
- Why can two parts with the same nominal dimension have slightly different measured sizes?
- Why does a drawing include tolerances?
- What is the difference between a dimension and a surface-finish requirement?
- Why should a student avoid declaring a part acceptable from one number alone?

## 2. Vocabulary

| English | Russian | Example |
|---|---|---|
| nominal dimension | номинальный размер | The nominal dimension is stated on the drawing. |
| actual size | действительный размер | The actual size is obtained by measurement. |
| tolerance | допуск | The tolerance defines permitted variation. |
| upper limit | верхний предел | The upper limit is part of the specified range. |
| lower limit | нижний предел | The lower limit is part of the specified range. |
| limit size | предельный размер | A limit size defines a boundary. |
| deviation | отклонение | The deviation is considered with the tolerance. |
| clearance fit | посадка с зазором | A clearance fit provides a positive gap in the assembled condition. |
| interference fit | посадка с натягом | An interference fit involves overlap in the specified mating dimensions. |
| transition fit | переходная посадка | A transition fit may result in clearance or interference, according to the limits. |
| surface finish | качество / шероховатость поверхности (по контексту) | The drawing specifies the surface finish. |
| surface texture | текстура поверхности | Surface texture is described using defined parameters. |
| roughness | шероховатость | Roughness is one aspect of surface texture. |
| specification | технические требования | Check the applicable specification. |
| acceptance criteria | критерии приёмки | Acceptance criteria must be supplied by the approved documentation. |

## 3. Reading: why tolerances matter

Read:

“A nominal dimension is the stated target size in a drawing or specification. A manufactured feature may have an actual measured size that differs from the nominal value. A tolerance defines the permitted variation for that feature. Upper and lower limits can be used to describe the acceptable size range.

Fits describe the dimensional relationship between mating parts. In a simplified explanation, a clearance fit provides space between the parts, an interference fit involves dimensional overlap, and a transition fit may produce either condition depending on the actual sizes. Surface-finish requirements describe the required surface texture and must be read with the drawing notes and specified measurement method.

A student cannot decide whether a part is acceptable from the nominal and measured values alone if the applicable tolerance, measurement method or acceptance criteria are missing.”

### First reading — gist

A) The text explains basic dimension, fit and surface-finish terms.  
B) The text provides a universal tolerance for every part.  
C) The text says measurements are unnecessary.

### Second reading — details

1. What does a nominal dimension represent?
2. What does a tolerance define?
3. What is the general idea of a clearance fit?
4. What is the general idea of an interference fit?
5. What does surface finish describe?
6. What additional information may be needed before an acceptance decision?

## 3. Reading: what makes a good handover?

Read:

“A workshop handover transfers relevant information from one person or team to another. A clear handover identifies the area or equipment, the time, the current status, completed checks, outstanding issues and the person responsible for follow-up. It separates observed facts from assumptions. For example, ‘A warning light was visible at 14:20’ is an observation. ‘The sensor is faulty’ is a diagnosis and should not be stated unless it has been confirmed by an authorised person.

If a student notices a hazard, an unexpected condition or an unclear instruction, the student should stop the activity when required by the local safety procedure, warn nearby people where appropriate and contact the instructor or responsible person. Students must not attempt unauthorised isolation, repair or restart. The college's approved workshop rules and emergency arrangements always take priority.”

### First reading — gist

A) The text explains factual handovers and safe reporting language.  
B) The text teaches students to repair equipment.  
C) The text says students should ignore warning signs.

### Second reading — details

1. Name four fields in a clear handover.
2. What is the difference between an observation and a diagnosis?
3. What should a student do if an instruction is unclear?
4. Who should be contacted about an abnormal condition?
5. What must students not attempt without authorisation?
6. Which rules take priority?

## 4. Language focus: clear stop-work and clarification phrases

Use short, direct sentences:
- **Stop, please. I have noticed an unexpected condition.**
- **I am not sure this is the current instruction.**
- **Could you confirm the equipment status?**
- **The warning light is visible.**
- **I have not checked the cause.**
- **Please contact the instructor.**
- **The issue remains unresolved.**
- **Do not restart until the responsible person has confirmed the next step.**

For a handover, use:
1. **Location/equipment:** where the issue occurred;
2. **Time:** when it was noticed;
3. **Observation:** what was seen or heard;
4. **Status:** what has and has not been done;

## 6. Listening: a shift handover

**Before you listen**

Key words: *warning indicator, observed, cause not confirmed, instructor, outstanding issue, handover*. Predict the information the next person needs.

**First listening — gist**

What is the purpose of the conversation?

A) To pass on an unresolved safety-related observation.  
B) To give permission to restart equipment.  
C) To discuss a sports result.

**Second listening — details**

1. Where was the observation made?
   A) Bay 2 B) Bay 12 C) Office 20
2. What time is recorded?
   A) 14:20 B) 04:12 C) 20:14
3. Has the cause been confirmed?
   A) No B) Yes C) The record says it is irrelevant
4. Who has been informed?
   A) the instructor B) a visitor C) nobody
5. Is the issue cleared in this exercise?
   A) No B) Yes C) Automatically

**Recording script**

**Student A:** I need to hand over an outstanding issue from Bay 2.

**Student B:** What happened?

**Student A:** At 14:20, I noticed a warning indicator on the training equipment. I stopped the student activity and informed the instructor.

**Student B:** Has the cause been identified?

**Student A:** No. It has not been confirmed, and I have not attempted to reset or repair anything.

**Student B:** Is the equipment cleared for use?

**Student A:** No. The record says the issue remains outstanding until the instructor reviews it under the local procedure.

**Student B:** I will include that status in the handover and contact the instructor before any further activity.

**Language focus**
- *I need to hand over…* — introducing an outstanding issue;
- *I noticed…* — reporting an observation;
- *has not been confirmed* — avoiding unsupported diagnosis;
- *remains outstanding* — reporting unresolved status;
- *before any further activity* — clear timing.

**After listening**

Role-play in groups of three: outgoing student, incoming student and instructor. The outgoing student reports only the facts; the incoming student repeats the status; the instructor confirms the next communication step. Do not simulate repair or restart.

## 7. Four professional viewpoints

- **Machine-tool operator:** report machine-area observations, tool/document status and outstanding checks.
- **Technologist:** communicate process-document discrepancies and unresolved quality questions.
- **Programmer:** hand over software/programme revision, data status and unresolved system messages without claiming a fault diagnosis unless confirmed.
- **Electrician:** communicate electrical observations and test-document references; any isolation, repair or electrical intervention belongs only to authorised personnel under local procedure.
