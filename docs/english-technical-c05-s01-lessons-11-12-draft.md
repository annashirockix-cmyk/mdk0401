**Status:** draft teaching materials for review; not imported into the portal.

# Lesson 11. Risk Assessment and Safety Reporting

**ID:** `en-c05-s01-l11`

### Learning outcomes
Students can distinguish a hazard, risk, consequence and control measure; describe a concern using neutral language; explain the purpose of a risk assessment at a general level; identify limitations in a fictional risk record; and write a factual safety report without inventing controls or risk ratings.

### 1. Lead-in
Discuss:
1. What is the difference between a hazard and a risk?
2. Why should a safety report describe evidence rather than blame a person?
3. Why can a risk rating not be copied from one workplace to another?
4. What should a student do if an instruction or work area appears unsafe?

### 3. Reading: Describe the concern; do not invent the decision

A hazard is a condition, activity or object with the potential to cause harm. Risk considers the possibility and potential consequences of harm in a particular context. A risk assessment is a structured process used by an organization to identify hazards, evaluate risk and decide what controls are appropriate.

The terminology is common, but the method is not universal. Organizations may use different risk matrices, categories, approval levels and reporting systems. A student should not invent a likelihood score, severity level or control measure. Those decisions belong to the approved process and the responsible competent people.

A useful safety report describes:
- what was observed or reported;
- where and when it happened, if known;
- which people or activities may be affected, without unnecessary personal details;
- what immediate communication or referral took place;
- what remains unknown.

A near miss is an event that could have caused harm but did not. Reporting near misses can help an organization identify patterns before a more serious event occurs. Reports should be factual and respectful. Avoid blame, speculation and unsupported statements about root cause.

If a student believes a classroom activity or work environment is unsafe, the appropriate response is to stop participating in the activity and promptly tell the teacher or responsible supervisor, following the institution’s safety arrangements. This lesson does not replace local safety training or emergency instructions.

**Fictional classroom safety report**

| Field | Training example |
|---|---|
| Reference | SAFE-TR-05 |
| Location | Simulated training area |
| Observation | A marked boundary was not visible in one photograph |
| Potential concern | A person might not recognize the intended restricted area |
| Immediate communication | The student informed the teacher |
| Risk rating | Not assigned in this language exercise |
| Follow-up | Teacher to review the classroom setup under local rules |

**Comprehension**
1. How does a hazard differ from risk?
2. Why should students not invent a risk rating?
3. What information belongs in a factual safety report?
4. What is a near miss?
5. What should a student do if a classroom activity appears unsafe?

### 4. Listening: Reporting a safety concern

**Before you listen**
- Review *hazard, risk, near miss, observation, follow-up*.
- Predict the difference between describing a concern and deciding its risk rating.
- Discuss why the speaker should avoid assigning blame.

**First listening — gist**
A. A student reports a fictional classroom concern and refers it to the teacher.
B. A student assigns an official risk score.
C. A student gives instructions for operating equipment.

**Second listening — details**
1. What was not visible in the photograph?
2. Where was the concern noticed?
3. Who was informed?
4. Was a risk rating assigned?
5. Who is responsible for the follow-up in the example?

**Recording script**

“I would like to report a concern from the simulated training area. In photograph SAFE-TR-05, the marked boundary is not visible. I cannot confirm from the photograph whether the boundary was missing in the room or simply outside the camera view. I informed the teacher and kept the original image with the classroom record. No risk rating has been assigned in this language exercise. The teacher will review the training setup under the institution’s local safety arrangements. I am reporting what I observed, not making a conclusion about the cause.”

**Transcript reveal and key**
- Gist: A.
- 1 The marked boundary.
- 2 The simulated training area.
- 3 The teacher.
- 4 No.
- 5 The teacher, under local safety arrangements.

### 5. Language focus: Cautious reporting and conditionals

Useful forms:
- *I observed that…*
- *The photograph appears to show…*
- *I cannot confirm whether…*
- *If the information is unclear, ask the responsible person.*
- *If a student notices an unsafe condition, they should report it.*
- *The cause has not been established.*

**Practice — choose the accurate wording**
1. The photograph does not show the boundary clearly.
   a) “The boundary was definitely removed.”  
   b) “The boundary is not visible in the photograph.”
2. The cause is unknown.
   a) “The cause has not been established.”  
   b) “The operator caused the incident.”
3. A student is unsure whether an activity is safe.
   a) “Continue and guess.”  
   b) “Stop participating and ask the teacher or responsible supervisor.”

**Key:** 1b, 2a, 3b.

**Grammar focus:** First conditional for general safety communication:
- *If the document is unclear, ask for clarification.*
- *If a concern is observed, report it promptly.*
- *If the cause is unknown, do not present a hypothesis as fact.*

### 6. Guided practice

**A. Classify the statement**
1. “A cable is visible across the floor in the training photograph.”
2. “Someone will definitely trip.”
3. “The image does not show whether the area was restricted.”
4. “The teacher has been informed.”

**Key:** 1 observation; 2 unsupported prediction; 3 limitation/uncertainty; 4 communication record.

**B. Complete a factual safety report**
- What was observed:
- Where and when:
- Source of information:
- Potential concern (carefully worded):
- Immediate communication:
- What is not known:
- Person/role to whom it was referred:

**C. Four professional routes**
- **Technologist:** report an unclear hazard label in a fictional process-training sheet; do not define a real control measure.
- **Programmer:** report a simulated interface message that could be misunderstood; distinguish the displayed message from the user’s interpretation.
- **Electrician:** report a missing identifier in a classroom electrical-system diagram; do not infer that a real circuit is unsafe.
- **CNC operator:** report that a fictional training-area boundary is not visible in a classroom image; do not provide machine-operation instructions.

### 7. Speaking and writing

**Speaking — safety report briefing.** Give a 60–90-second factual report. Include the observation, source, uncertainty, communication already made and next responsible role.

**Writing — incident/near-miss note.** Write 130–160 words about a fictional classroom scenario. Use neutral wording and at least three expressions of uncertainty. Do not invent a risk score, root cause or corrective action.

### 8. Self-check

1. A hazard is:
   a) something with potential to cause harm; b) always an injury; c) a completed report.
2. Risk considers:
   a) likelihood and consequences in context; b) the writer’s guess only; c) file size.
3. A student should invent a risk score if the form is blank:
   a) yes; b) no; c) only if the event is minor.
4. A near miss:
   a) could have caused harm but did not; b) is always a confirmed injury; c) is an approved work instruction.
5. A factual report should:
   a) distinguish observation from assumption; b) assign blame; c) hide uncertainty.

**Key:** 1a, 2a, 3b, 4a, 5a.

**Independent work:** Create a fictional safety-report form with seven fields. Write an 80–100-word sample report and highlight the sentence that states what is not known.

# Lesson 12. Integrated Technical Briefing: From Task to Report

**ID:** `en-c05-s01-l12`

### Learning outcomes
Students can structure a professional technical briefing from a task description to a final report; summarize evidence from documents, tables and team contributions; distinguish scope, method, findings and limitations; answer clarification questions; and produce a coherent written report for a multidisciplinary audience.

### 1. Lead-in
A technical task often involves more than one document and more than one specialist. Discuss:
1. What should a briefing explain before presenting results?
2. How can a speaker make technical information understandable to a mixed audience?
3. Why must limitations be included in the final report?
4. What is the difference between a finding and a recommendation?

### 2. Key vocabulary

| English | Russian | Example |
|---|---|---|
| task brief | описание / постановка задачи | The task brief defines the scope. |
| scope | объём и границы задачи | The scope excludes operational work. |
| objective | цель | The objective is to review a training information pack. |
| methodology | методика, общий подход | The methodology explains how information was reviewed. |
| source material | исходные материалы | The report lists the source material. |
| finding | установленный результат анализа | A finding is supported by evidence. |
| limitation | ограничение | The report identifies its limitations. |
| recommendation | рекомендация | A recommendation should follow from the findings. |
| executive summary | краткое резюме | The executive summary presents the main points. |
| deliverable | итоговый результат | The final report is the main deliverable. |
| appendix | приложение | The appendix contains supporting tables. |
| cross-reference | перекрёстная ссылка | A cross-reference links related records. |
| consistent | согласованный, непротиворечивый | The terminology is consistent across the report. |
| evidence-based | основанный на данных | The conclusion is evidence-based. |
| present findings | представлять результаты | The team will present its findings. |
| follow-up action | последующее действие | Each follow-up action has an owner. |

### 3. Reading: From a task brief to a defensible report

A technical briefing is more than a list of facts. It guides the audience through the task, the information reviewed, the findings and the next steps. A clear structure helps the audience understand what was done and what was not done.

A useful briefing can follow this sequence:

1. **Task and objective:** What question was the team asked to address?
2. **Scope:** What is included and excluded?
3. **Source material:** Which documents, records or data were reviewed?
4. **Approach:** How was the information compared or organized?
5. **Findings:** What can be stated on the basis of the evidence?
6. **Limitations:** What is missing, uncertain or outside the task?
7. **Recommendations / follow-up:** What should be clarified, by whom, under which approved process?
8. **Conclusion:** What is the overall status?

A finding must be supported by a source. A recommendation should not go beyond the evidence or the authority of the team. If the task is a classroom document review, the conclusion must remain a classroom document-review conclusion. It cannot be expanded into a statement about an aircraft’s airworthiness, a real manufacturing release or authorization to operate equipment.

**Fictional integrated task brief**

**Project:** Training Information Pack Review — TP-15  
**Objective:** Review consistency and clarity across four classroom documents.  
**Scope:** Document references, labels, sample table and written handover.  
**Excluded:** Physical inspection, equipment operation, aircraft maintenance, production approval and real risk assessment.

**Source material**
- Process overview TP-12, revision A.
- Sample data dictionary DD-03, revision B.
- Classroom system glossary EG-02, revision A.
- Training drawing DR-08, revision C.

**Fictional findings**
- One process-sheet note requires clarification.
- One data field has inconsistent labels in two classroom files.
- Two glossary terms are used for the same sample concept.
- The feature identifier is absent from the training drawing example.
- The sample trend table contains six rows and arbitrary index units only.

**Limitations**
- The source pack is fictional.
- No operational or physical equipment data were reviewed.
- No root cause, risk score, acceptance decision or maintenance status was established.

### 4. Listening: Presenting an integrated technical briefing

**Before you listen**
- Review the eight stages in the reading.
- Predict which limitations should be stated.
- Prepare to identify findings and follow-up actions separately.

**First listening — gist**
A. A team presents the scope, evidence, findings and limitations of a fictional document review.
B. A team reports that an aircraft has been approved for service.
C. A team gives instructions for modifying equipment.

**Second listening — details**
1. What is the project reference?
2. What is the objective?
3. Name two findings from the document review.
4. What is excluded from the scope?
5. What does the team recommend as the next step?

**Recording script**

“Good morning. This briefing summarizes the fictional Training Information Pack Review, reference TP-15. Our objective was to review the clarity and consistency of four classroom documents. We compared their references, labels and sample information. The review identified one process-sheet note that needs clarification and one data field with inconsistent labels. We also found two terms for the same sample concept in the glossary, while the training drawing example does not show its feature identifier. The scope excluded physical inspection, equipment operation, production approval and aircraft maintenance. The source pack contains fictional information only. We recommend recording each finding, assigning a document owner to clarify it and updating the handover after confirmation. No operational status or approval decision has been made.”

**Transcript reveal and key**
- Gist: A.
- 1 TP-15.
- 2 Review clarity and consistency across four classroom documents.
- 3 Any two: process-sheet note needs clarification; data field labels inconsistent; glossary terms differ; drawing feature identifier absent.
- 4 Physical inspection, equipment operation, production approval and aircraft maintenance.
- 5 Record each finding, assign a document owner and update the handover after confirmation.

### 5. Language focus: Signposting and reporting findings

Use signposting phrases to guide the audience:
- *I will begin by outlining the objective.*
- *The scope of this review was limited to…*
- *The source material consisted of…*
- *The first finding is…*
- *A further point concerns…*
- *It is important to note that…*
- *The available evidence does not establish…*
- *We recommend that the document owner…*
- *To conclude…*

**Grammar: recommend + that-clause / recommend + -ing**
- *We recommend that the document owner review the label.*
- *We recommend reviewing the label with the document owner.*

**Practice — complete the briefing**
1. “The scope of this review was limited ______ four classroom documents.” (to / for)
2. “The available evidence does not ______ the cause.” (establish / establishes)
3. “We recommend that the document owner ______ the reference.” (confirm / confirms)
4. “A further point ______ the missing identifier.” (concerns / concern)

**Key:** 1 to; 2 establish; 3 confirm; 4 concerns.

### 6. Guided practice

**A. Put the briefing sections in order**
- Findings
- Objective
- Conclusion
- Source material and approach
- Scope and limitations
- Follow-up actions

**Suggested order:** Objective → Scope and limitations → Source material and approach → Findings → Follow-up actions → Conclusion.

**B. Finding or recommendation?**
1. “The sample glossary contains two terms for the same concept.”
2. “The glossary owner should confirm the preferred term.”
3. “The drawing example does not show the feature identifier.”
4. “The document owner should review the missing identifier.”

**Key:** 1 finding; 2 recommendation; 3 finding; 4 recommendation.

**C. Four professional routes**
Each group member contributes one 30-second section:
- **Technologist:** describe the process-document finding and its source.
- **Programmer:** explain the sample data-label discrepancy and its limitation.
- **Electrician:** describe the terminology mismatch in the classroom glossary.
- **CNC operator:** explain the missing feature identifier in the training drawing.
The group then combines the four sections into one briefing. No student may claim to approve a real technical configuration.

### 7. Speaking and writing

**Speaking — final briefing.** Deliver a 3–4-minute presentation using the integrated task brief. Every speaker must:
- state their role and source;
- describe one evidence-based finding;
- identify one limitation;
- answer one clarification question;
- hand over to the next speaker with a clear transition.

**Writing — final technical report.** Write 220–260 words with:
- title and reference;
- objective and scope;
- source material;
- method of review;
- at least three findings;
- limitations;
- follow-up actions and owners;
- concise conclusion.

### 8. Self-check

1. The scope defines:
   a) the boundaries of the task; b) the author’s hobbies; c) the final font.
2. A finding should be:
   a) supported by evidence; b) invented to complete the report; c) unrelated to the objective.
3. A limitation explains:
   a) what the task or evidence does not cover; b) why the writer is always correct; c) a confirmed root cause.
4. A recommendation should:
   a) follow from the findings and remain within authority; b) replace evidence; c) approve every action.
5. A good briefing uses:
   a) clear signposting; b) unexplained abbreviations only; c) unsupported certainty.

**Key:** 1a, 2a, 3a, 4a, 5a.

**Independent work:** Prepare a 3-minute presentation and a 220-word report about a fictional technical information pack. Use at least four sources, three findings, two limitations and two follow-up actions. Include a short glossary for a non-specialist audience.
