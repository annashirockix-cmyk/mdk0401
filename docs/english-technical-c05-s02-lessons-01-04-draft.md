# Lesson 1. Delivering a Technical Presentation

**ID:** `en-c05-s02-l01`  
**Suggested level:** B2  
**Suggested duration:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, students can:
- plan a short technical presentation for a mixed professional audience;
- introduce a topic, state an objective and explain the scope;
- use signposting language to guide listeners;
- describe evidence from a table or document without exaggeration;
- answer clarification questions and acknowledge limitations;
- deliver a structured 3–4-minute presentation.

## 1. Lead-in: audience and purpose

A technical presentation is successful when the audience understands the purpose, the evidence and the limits of the conclusion. A presentation to specialists may use precise terminology; a presentation to a mixed team needs short explanations of less familiar terms.

Discuss:
1. What makes a technical presentation easy to follow?
2. Why should a speaker explain the purpose before showing data?
3. How can a speaker make a complex term clear to a non-specialist?
4. What should a presenter say when the available information is incomplete?

### Useful pronunciation

Pay attention to word stress:
- ob**JEC**tive;
- **E**vidence;
- limi**TA**tion;
- clarifi**CA**tion;
- pre**SEN**tation (noun) / pre**SENT** (verb).

## 3. Reading: a presentation with a clear route

A useful technical presentation follows a predictable structure:

1. **Opening:** greet the audience and introduce the topic.
2. **Purpose:** explain why the presentation is being delivered.
3. **Scope:** state what is included and excluded.
4. **Method and sources:** identify the documents, sample data or observations reviewed.
5. **Findings:** present the results in a logical order.
6. **Limitations:** explain what the evidence cannot establish.
7. **Conclusion:** summarize the main message.
8. **Questions and handover:** invite questions and identify the next speaker or responsible role.

The speaker should not read every word from a slide. Slides support the spoken explanation; they do not replace it. A slide should normally contain a short heading, a few key points, a clear visual and a source note where appropriate.

A good presenter distinguishes between a fact, an interpretation and a recommendation. For example:

- Fact: “The training table contains twelve records.”
- Interpretation: “Two records use a different label.”
- Recommendation: “The data owner should confirm the preferred label.”

The interpretation must be reasonable, and the recommendation must follow from the evidence. A small classroom sample cannot establish the performance of a real aircraft system or an entire production process.

### Fictional briefing context

**Project:** Aviation Training Data Review — ATD-08  
**Audience:** technologist, programmer, electrician and CNC operator  
**Objective:** explain inconsistencies in a fictional classroom information pack  
**Sources:** sample process sheet, data dictionary, wiring-diagram glossary and CNC drawing extract  
**Findings:** one inconsistent term, one missing identifier and two records with incomplete units  
**Limitations:** no physical equipment, real aircraft, live production data or operational procedure was examined.

# Lesson 2. Writing a Structured Technical Report

**ID:** `en-c05-s02-l02`  
**Suggested level:** B2  
**Suggested duration:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students can:
- distinguish a report from an informal message;
- organize a technical report into standard sections;
- describe evidence objectively;
- use passive forms and cautious language where appropriate;
- summarize a table and identify missing information;
- edit a report for clarity, consistency and traceability.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
1. Why are technical reports written?
2. Which details help another specialist understand what was reviewed?
3. Why should a report include limitations?
4. What is the difference between “observed”, “measured”, “assumed” and “confirmed”?

## 2. Key vocabulary

| Term | Russian meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| report title | название отчёта | The report title identifies the subject. |
| reference number | регистрационный номер | Use the assigned reference number. |
| executive summary | краткое резюме | The executive summary gives the main result. |
| background | исходная информация | The background explains the context. |
| objective | цель | The objective defines the question. |
| scope | объём и границы | The scope excludes physical testing. |
| methodology | методика / способ анализа | The methodology describes the review. |
| source record | исходная запись | Each source record is listed. |
| observation | наблюдение | The observation is recorded without interpretation. |
| finding | результат | The finding is linked to evidence. |
| non-conformity | несоответствие | The report describes a sample non-conformity. |
| corrective action | корректирующее действие | The action must be assigned by an authorized role. |
| appendix | приложение | The raw table is included in the appendix. |
| traceability | прослеживаемость | Traceability links a finding to its source. |
| revision history | история редакций | The revision history lists changes. |

## 3. Reading: anatomy of a technical report

A structured report allows another person to understand the work without relying on an informal conversation. A report should identify its purpose, scope, sources and findings. It should also state what was not examined.

A common structure is:

1. **Title and reference:** subject, document ID, author or team and date.
2. **Executive summary:** a short account of the purpose, main findings and status.
3. **Background:** why the review was requested.
4. **Objective and scope:** what question was addressed and what was excluded.
5. **Sources and method:** documents, records and comparison method.
6. **Results:** observations and findings, preferably numbered.
7. **Discussion:** what the findings may mean and what remains uncertain.
8. **Limitations:** missing data, small sample, unavailable records or excluded activities.
9. **Conclusion:** a concise statement supported by the report.
10. **Follow-up:** questions, responsible roles and expected document updates.
11. **Appendices:** tables, glossary, figures and supporting records.

A report should not turn an assumption into a fact. “The label is absent from the sample” is an observation. “The system is unsafe” is a much broader conclusion and cannot be supported by that observation alone.

## 5. Language focus: objective reporting

### Passive voice

Passive forms are common when the process or result is more important than the person performing it:

- “The records were compared.”
- “The missing label was identified.”
- “The source document is listed in Appendix A.”
- “The report has been reviewed.”

Do not use passive voice to hide responsibility where the responsible role is known and relevant.

### Cautious language

- “The sample suggests that…”
- “The available records indicate…”
- “This may be related to…”
- “The cause has not been established.”
- “Further confirmation is required.”
- “The report does not establish…”

### Exercise A — correct the statements

1. “The records compared yesterday.”  
2. “The missing label identify in row R-04.”  
3. “The sample prove that the whole system is unreliable.”  
4. “The source reference not included.”

**Suggested corrections:**
1. The records were compared yesterday.
2. The missing label was identified in row R-04.
3. The sample does not prove that the whole system is unreliable.
4. The source reference was not included.

### Exercise B — classify

- “Row R-04 has no label in file B.” — observation.
- “The two files may use different naming conventions.” — cautious interpretation.
- “The data owner should confirm the preferred label.” — recommendation.
- “The entire information system is defective.” — unsupported conclusion from this sample.

## 6. Four professional routes

Create one short report paragraph for each route:

- **Technologist:** compare two fictional process-sheet labels; state the source revision.
- **Programmer:** describe a missing field in a sample software test log.
- **Electrician:** report an unclear symbol description in a classroom diagram.
- **CNC operator:** describe a missing feature reference in a fictional drawing extract.

Use: reference → observation → evidence → limitation → follow-up.

## 7. Speaking and writing

**Speaking:** explain a report finding to a colleague in 90 seconds. The listener asks what evidence supports it.

**Writing:** write a 180–220-word report using the structure in the reading. Include at least three numbered findings and two limitations.

## 8. Self-check

1. An executive summary:
   A. gives the main points; B. contains every raw record; C. replaces the evidence.
2. Traceability links:
   A. findings to source records; B. names to hobbies; C. colours to slide themes.
3. A report should:
   A. distinguish observation from interpretation; B. exaggerate the result; C. omit the scope.
4. Passive voice:
   A. can emphasize a process or result; B. always removes the need for an actor; C. proves a cause.
5. A limitation describes:
   A. a boundary of the evidence or task; B. a confirmed defect; C. an approval.

**Key:** 1 A; 2 A; 3 A; 4 A; 5 A.

**Independent work:** Prepare a report and an appendix containing a five-row fictional evidence table. Check that every finding can be traced to a row or document reference.

# Lesson 3. Professional Interview and Oral Examination Skills

**ID:** `en-c05-s02-l03`  
**Suggested level:** B2  
**Suggested duration:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students can:
- answer technical questions with a clear structure;
- explain a process or document without claiming unsupported expertise;
- ask for clarification and check that a question has been understood;
- describe a problem using evidence and limitations;
- respond appropriately when they do not know an answer;
- participate in a structured oral examination or professional interview.

## 1. Lead-in: answering under pressure

Discuss:
1. Why can a technically correct answer still be difficult to understand?
2. What can you say if you need the examiner to repeat a question?
3. Is it better to guess or explain what information is missing?
4. How can you give an example without making a universal claim?

## 2. Key vocabulary and phrases

| Phrase | Function |
|---|---|
| “Could you repeat the last part?” | ask for repetition |
| “Do you mean the document revision or the software version?” | clarify the question |
| “I would approach this by…” | structure an explanation |
| “The first point to consider is…” | introduce a point |
| “For example…” | give an example |
| “The available evidence shows…” | refer to evidence |
| “I cannot confirm that from the information provided.” | acknowledge a limit |
| “I would check the approved source before answering.” | identify a responsible next step |
| “To summarize…” | conclude |
| “May I add one point?” | contribute politely |

Additional vocabulary:
- oral examination — устный экзамен;
- interviewer — интервьюер;
- candidate — кандидат;
- follow-up question — дополнительный вопрос;
- evidence — доказательство / данные;
- assumption — предположение;
- competence — компетенция;
- relevant experience — соответствующий опыт;
- concise answer — краткий и точный ответ;
- professional judgement — профессиональное суждение.

## 3. Reading: a reliable answer structure

A clear oral answer can follow the **PREP** structure:

- **P — Point:** answer the question directly.
- **R — Reason:** explain why.
- **E — Example:** give a relevant example.
- **P — Point:** summarize the main message.

Example question: “Why is document revision important?”

Answer:
“Document revision is important because a team must know which version is being discussed. If two people use different revisions, their references may not match. For example, in a fictional classroom exercise, a drawing extract and a process sheet showed different revision letters. The team therefore recorded the mismatch and requested confirmation. In short, revision control supports clear communication and traceability.”

A candidate should not pretend to know a fact that has not been provided. A professional answer can say what is known, what is not known and what source would be needed. This is stronger than an unsupported guess.

### Common interview question types

1. **Definition:** “What is traceability?”
2. **Explanation:** “How does a report support communication?”
3. **Comparison:** “What is the difference between an observation and a conclusion?”
4. **Scenario:** “What would you do if two documents showed different revisions?”
5. **Reflection:** “Which skill would you like to improve?”
6. **Evidence question:** “Which record supports your statement?”

### Recording script

**Examiner:** Why is revision control important in a technical environment?

**Candidate:** Revision control helps a team identify the version of a document being used. It reduces confusion when information is updated.

**Examiner:** Can you give an example?

**Candidate:** Yes. In a fictional classroom case, a drawing extract showed revision C, while a related process sheet referred to revision B. The difference was recorded as an open question.

**Examiner:** Does that prove that one document is incorrect?

**Candidate:** Not by itself. We would need to check the controlled document register and confirm which revision applies to the task. The cause of the mismatch has not been established.

**Examiner:** What would you do next?

**Candidate:** I would record the two references, avoid making an unsupported choice and ask the responsible document owner to confirm the current version. I would then update the handover with the confirmed information.

# Lesson 4. Reading Specifications, Procedures and Standards

**ID:** `en-c05-s02-l04`  
**Suggested level:** B2  
**Suggested duration:** 90 minutes

## Learning outcomes

Students can:
- identify the purpose and structure of a specification, procedure and standard;
- distinguish requirements, recommendations, permissions and prohibitions;
- interpret modal verbs and obligation language;
- locate scope, revision, definitions and referenced documents;
- identify when a document is incomplete or outside the task;
- describe why current approved sources must be checked by the responsible role.

## 1. Lead-in

Discuss:
1. What is the difference between a specification and a procedure?
2. Why do technical documents have revision identifiers?
3. Do the words “must”, “should” and “may” mean the same thing?
4. Why should a student avoid treating a classroom example as an approved work instruction?

## 2. Key vocabulary

| Term | Russian meaning | Example |
|---|---|---|
| specification | спецификация / технические требования | The specification defines the required characteristics. |
| procedure | процедура / порядок действий | The procedure describes an approved sequence. |
| standard | стандарт | A standard provides agreed requirements or guidance. |
| requirement | требование | Each requirement should be identifiable. |
| scope | область применения | The scope states where the document applies. |
| applicability | применимость | Applicability must be checked for the task. |
| revision | редакция | The revision is shown on the title page. |
| controlled copy | контролируемая копия | The controlled copy is held in the document system. |
| reference | ссылка / ссылка на документ | The reference identifies another source. |
| shall | должен / обязан (требование в нормативном стиле) | “Shall” commonly expresses a requirement in standards. |

## 3. Reading: reading technical documents carefully

Technical documents are written for different purposes.

- A **specification** describes required characteristics, interfaces, performance or acceptance criteria.
- A **procedure** describes an approved sequence or method for a defined task.
- A **standard** establishes agreed requirements, definitions or recommended practices within its stated scope.
- A **manual** provides information about a product, system or subject.
- A **record** documents what happened, what was observed or what was measured.

Before using a document, a reader should identify:
1. title and document ID;
2. revision and issue date;
3. scope and applicability;
4. definitions and abbreviations;
5. referenced documents;
6. requirement wording;
7. approval or release status, where relevant.

Words such as *shall*, *should*, *may* and *must not* carry different meanings. Their exact interpretation can depend on the document's governing rules, so readers must follow the definitions and conventions stated in the actual controlled document.

A student should not infer that a document is current because a file name looks recent. The applicable source and revision must be confirmed through the authorized document-control process. A classroom excerpt is useful for language practice, but it is not a substitute for a current approved procedure.

## 5. Language focus: requirements and recommendations

### Modal language

- **shall** — commonly expresses a formal requirement in standards and specifications;
- **must** — expresses strong obligation in many contexts;
- **should** — commonly expresses a recommendation;
- **may** — can express permission or possibility;
- **must not** — expresses prohibition;
- **is required to** — expresses obligation;
- **is permitted to** — expresses permission;
- **is not applicable to** — states that a requirement does not apply within the defined context.

### Exercise A — identify the function

1. “The record shall include a reference.” — requirement.
2. “The source revision should be stated.” — recommendation.
3. “The learner may use the glossary.” — permission.
4. “The learner must not treat the extract as an approved procedure.” — prohibition.
5. “This section is not applicable to the classroom data set.” — applicability statement.

### Exercise B — rewrite carefully

1. “You can use the glossary.”  
   Suggested formal version: “The learner may use the supplied glossary.”
2. “It is a good idea to list the revision.”  
   Suggested version: “The source revision should be stated.”
3. “The record has to contain an ID.”  
   Suggested version: “The record shall include a unique reference.”  
   *Note: this is a language transformation for the fictional exercise, not a real standard.*
