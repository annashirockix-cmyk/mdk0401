# Lesson 07. Education Systems and Lifelong Learning

**Suggested time:** 80–90 minutes  
**Main aim:** describe education, compare ways of learning and explain why people continue learning throughout their working lives.

## Learning outcomes

By the end of the lesson, students can:
1. name common stages and forms of education;
2. distinguish a school, a college, a university and vocational training;
3. use `present perfect` with `for` and `since` in familiar learning contexts;
4. describe a skill they have developed and a skill they want to improve;
5. understand the main idea and key details of a short conversation about further training.

## 1. Lead-in: learning never stops

Discuss:
- What subjects did you enjoy at school?
- What can you learn at college that you could not learn at school?
- Can a person learn a new profession as an adult?
- Which is more useful: learning from a teacher, from a book, or by doing a task?

**Key idea:** education is not limited to childhood. People learn at school, at college, at work and independently. New equipment, software, materials and safety requirements make continuous professional development important.

## 2. Vocabulary: stages and forms of education

| English | Russian | Example |
|---|---|---|
| primary education | начальное образование | Primary education usually starts at an early age. |
| secondary education | среднее образование | Students complete secondary education at school. |
| vocational education | профессиональное образование | Vocational education combines theory and practice. |
| college | колледж | She studies engineering at college. |
| higher education | высшее образование | Higher education may include a university degree. |
| qualification | квалификация | The course leads to a professional qualification. |
| apprenticeship | обучение на рабочем месте / ученичество | An apprenticeship combines work and training. |
| practical training | практическое обучение | Practical training develops job-related skills. |
| online course | онлайн-курс | I have completed an online course in safety. |
| lifelong learning | обучение в течение всей жизни | Lifelong learning helps people adapt to change. |
| update your skills | обновлять навыки | Technicians update their skills when equipment changes. |
| assessment | оценивание, проверка | The final assessment includes a practical task. |

**Word building**
- educate → education → educational
- qualify → qualification → qualified
- train → training → trainee / trainer
- employ → employment → employee / employer

Notice the suffixes:
- `-tion` often forms a noun: educate → education;
- `-al` often forms an adjective: education → educational;
- `-ed` can describe a person's state or qualification: qualified.

## 3. Grammar focus: Present Perfect with for and since

We use the Present Perfect to connect a past action or situation with the present.

**Form:**  
`have / has + past participle`

- I have studied English for two years.
- She has worked with computer systems since September.
- We have learned several ways to measure a component.

Use **for** with a period of time:
- for two years;
- for six months;
- for a long time.

Use **since** with the starting point:
- since 2024;
- since September;
- since I started college.

| Subject | Positive | Negative | Question |
|---|---|---|---|
| I / you / we / they | have studied | have not studied | Have you studied? |
| he / she / it | has studied | has not studied | Has she studied? |

**Past Simple or Present Perfect?**
- *I started college in 2025.* — a finished past event, with a finished time.
- *I have studied here for one year.* — the period continues up to now.

Common error: `I am studying English since September.`  
Better: `I have been studying English since September.` (If the course focuses only on Present Perfect Simple, `I have studied English since September` is also possible.)

## 4. Reading: A changing profession

Read the text. First identify its main idea; then answer the questions.

### Learning after graduation

When people choose a profession, they do not learn everything they will need for the rest of their lives in one course. Technology changes, companies introduce new equipment, and professional standards are updated. For this reason, many specialists continue their education after graduation.

A manufacturing technician may learn to work with a new measuring system. An electrician may take a course on updated electrical safety requirements. A programmer may study a new programming language or a method of checking data. A machine-tool operator may receive additional training before using a new CNC control system.

Learning can take different forms. Some people attend short courses at a training centre. Others study online, read technical manuals or learn from experienced colleagues. Practical experience is important, but it should be combined with reliable information and safe working procedures.

Lifelong learning does not mean studying every minute. It means being ready to identify a gap in your knowledge and take sensible steps to improve. A specialist who can learn, ask questions and check information is better prepared for changes at work.

# Lesson 08. Professions, Skills and Career Choices

**Suggested time:** 80–90 minutes  
**Main aim:** describe professions, responsibilities and personal skills; explain a career choice using reasons and examples.

## Learning outcomes

Students can:
1. name common engineering and technical professions;
2. distinguish a job title, a responsibility, a skill and a qualification;
3. use `can`, `be able to`, `have to` and `need to` to describe ability and duties;
4. understand a short conversation about choosing a technical career;
5. give a short structured description of a profession.

## 1. Lead-in: what makes a good specialist?

Discuss:
- What does a technologist do?
- How is a programmer's work different from an electrician's work?
- What skills do CNC operators need?
- Which skills are useful in all four professions?

**Transferable skills:** communication, teamwork, attention to detail, problem-solving, time management, responsibility and willingness to learn.

## 2. Vocabulary: professions and responsibilities

| Profession | Typical responsibility | Useful skills |
|---|---|---|
| process technologist | prepares and improves production processes | planning, reading process sheets, quality awareness |
| programmer | writes, tests and maintains software or control logic | logical thinking, debugging, data analysis |
| electrician | installs, inspects and tests electrical systems within their authorisation | reading diagrams, measurement, safety awareness |
| CNC machine operator | sets up and operates machine tools under approved procedures | drawing interpretation, measurement, concentration |
| quality inspector | checks products against specified requirements | accuracy, recording results, impartiality |
| maintenance technician | carries out approved maintenance tasks | fault reporting, tool knowledge, documentation |

**Important distinctions**
- A **responsibility** is something a person is expected to do.
- A **skill** is an ability developed through learning and practice.
- A **qualification** is recognised evidence that a person has met defined requirements.
- **Experience** is knowledge or ability gained by doing something over time.

### Practice 1. Match the terms

1. attention to detail  
2. troubleshoot  
3. maintain  
4. interpret a drawing  
5. record measurements

a) write down measured values;  
b) find and investigate the cause of a problem;  
c) understand information shown in a technical drawing;  
d) notice small but important differences;  
e) keep equipment in the required condition through approved work.

## 3. Grammar focus: ability, obligation and necessity

### Can / can't

Use **can** for present ability or possibility:
- A programmer can write a simple script.
- A student can't operate equipment without the required training.

### Be able to

Use **be able to** when another tense or form is needed:
- After the course, she will be able to read the diagram.
- He has been able to improve his technical vocabulary.

### Have to

Use **have to** for an obligation or requirement:
- Operators have to follow the approved setup procedure.
- Students have to wear the required protective equipment in the workshop.

### Need to

Use **need to** for something necessary:
- We need to check the drawing revision.
- A technician needs to record the test result.

**Negative meaning matters**
- `You don't have to attend` = attendance is not necessary.
- `You mustn't attend` = attendance is prohibited.

## 4. Reading: Four people, four technical careers

Read the profiles and complete the task.

### A. A process technologist
Elena works with production documentation. She helps prepare process sheets, checks whether the selected sequence is suitable for the part, and communicates with quality inspectors. She needs to understand materials, dimensions and manufacturing methods. Accuracy is important because a small error in a process document can affect the next production stage.

### B. A programmer
Timur develops small applications for collecting and displaying equipment data. He checks input values, tests changes and documents software behaviour. He has to think logically and explain technical problems clearly. He also works with electricians and technologists because software often receives information from physical equipment.

### C. An electrician
Rashid studies electrical diagrams and performs authorised inspection and measurement tasks. He records results and reports anything unusual. He must follow safety procedures and must not guess when a circuit reference or test condition is unclear. Communication is essential when work is handed over to another specialist.

### D. A CNC machine operator
Irina prepares the work area, checks the job documentation and uses measuring instruments to inspect a first-off part. She needs to understand dimensions, tolerances and tool information. She follows the approved setup and safety instructions and reports a mismatch instead of making an unauthorised change.

# Lesson 09. A First CV: Personal Profile and Work Experience

**Suggested time:** 90 minutes  
**Main aim:** understand the structure of a simple CV and prepare an honest, clear first CV for a student or entry-level applicant.

## Learning outcomes

Students can:
1. identify the main sections of a basic CV;
2. distinguish education, skills, projects and work experience;
3. write concise statements using action verbs;
4. use Past Simple for completed activities and Present Simple for current skills;
5. prepare a short personal profile and a first CV without inventing qualifications or experience.

## 1. Lead-in: what does an employer need to know?

Discuss:
- Why might a student need a CV before their first full-time job?
- What can a student include if they have no paid work experience?
- Why should a CV be accurate?
- Which information should not be included in a public CV?

A first CV can include education, practical training, coursework, projects, digital skills, languages and relevant personal strengths. Do not invent employment, certificates or levels of competence. Follow local rules and the employer's instructions about personal data.

## 2. Vocabulary: CV sections

| Section | Purpose | Example |
|---|---|---|
| contact details | how the applicant can be contacted | email address, telephone number |
| personal profile | a short professional introduction | “A college student interested in aircraft manufacturing…” |
| education | school, college and dates | “Technical College, 2024–present” |
| work experience | paid or formal work | “Workshop assistant, summer 2025” |
| practical training | supervised learning in a workplace or workshop | “Completed supervised measurement practice” |
| projects | relevant tasks completed during study | “Prepared a simple CNC process-planning project” |
| technical skills | tools, software and methods the applicant can use | “Basic CAD; spreadsheet skills” |
| languages | languages and honest proficiency descriptions | “English: elementary / intermediate” |
| references | people who may provide a reference, if requested | “Available on request” |

**Action verbs for a CV**
- assisted — помогал;
- prepared — подготовил;
- checked — проверил;
- measured — измерил;
- recorded — записал;
- designed — разработал / спроектировал;
- tested — протестировал;
- analysed — проанализировал;
- completed — выполнил;
- worked with — работал с.

Use specific verbs. “Did many things” is weak. “Recorded measurement results in a practice worksheet” is clearer.

## 3. Grammar and style: concise CV statements

### Present Simple for current skills and facts
- I study electrical engineering.
- I can read basic circuit diagrams.
- I use spreadsheets to organise data.

### Past Simple for completed projects and practice
- I completed a workshop safety module.
- I prepared a simple CAD drawing.
- I recorded the results of a supervised measurement task.

### Avoid unsupported claims
Weak: `I am the best programmer and expert in aircraft systems.`  
Better: `I am a college student developing basic programming and data-analysis skills.`

Weak: `I know all CNC machines.`  
Better: `I have practised reading basic CNC program blocks in class.`

### Useful profile structure

1. Who you are: `I am a second-year college student...`
2. Your area of interest: `...interested in aircraft electrical systems.`
3. Relevant skills: `I can read basic diagrams and record measurement results.`
4. Your goal: `I am looking for a supervised work-placement opportunity.`

### Practice 1. Improve the statements

Rewrite each statement to make it clear, specific and honest.

1. “I am good at computers.”
2. “I did a project.”
3. “I know machines.”
4. “I speak English very well.” (Assume the student is at A2 level.)
5. “I worked in a factory.” (Assume this was a two-day supervised college visit, not employment.)

**Recording script — reveal after the tasks**

**Tutor:** Have you started your CV for the work-placement application?  
**Nina:** Yes, but I don't have any work experience. I’m not sure what to write.  
**Tutor:** You can include relevant college projects and supervised practical training. What have you done this year?

**Nina:** Should I include my English level?

**Language focus**
- `I don't have any work experience` — a present fact.
- `We measured sample parts` — a completed activity, Past Simple.
- `Describe exactly what you did` — a direct instruction.
- `what kind of placement you are looking for` — a specific goal.
